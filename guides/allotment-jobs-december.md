December on the allotment is quieter, but it isn't idle. There are still crops to lift, brassicas to look after, fruit trees to prune and a shed that could do with some attention before spring. Below is a practical run-through of the month's jobs, based on advice from the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) and the National Allotment Society (NAS), followed by a handful of small gift ideas for the grower in your life.

## What's still worth harvesting in December

The RHS December list of vegetables ready to harvest includes parsnips, leeks, Brussels sprouts, swedes and cabbages, along with beetroot, turnips and celery ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)). For many plot holders this is the season of sprouts on the stalk and parsnips for the Christmas roast.

There's no need to rush the roots out of the ground. The NAS notes that swedes and parsnips "benefit from a few hard frosts to convert starches into sugars, thus sweetening them" ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)). It also suggests it is "generally good practice to have a few lifted and dry stored" in case the ground freezes too hard to dig them as needed, and to "regularly check all stored crops and remove any that show signs of decay" ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)).

If you store roots in trenches, the RHS suggests covering them with a protective layer of cardboard when hard frosts are forecast, so you can still get at them during cold snaps ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)).

Celery still in the ground may benefit from protection such as a covering of straw, according to the NAS ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)).

## Protecting crops: brassicas, pigeons and frost

Winter brassicas are a favourite with pigeons once other food runs short. The RHS advises protecting brassicas from pigeons "using cloches, re-used netting or biodegradable or re-used fleece" ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)). It's worth walking round the nets after a windy spell, as a gap at ground level is all a determined pigeon needs.

While you're there, the RHS recommends removing any yellowed leaves on Brussels sprouts and other brassicas, which it says will help prevent grey mould and brassica downy mildew ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)). Leggy sprout stalks that look vulnerable to wind rock can be staked or earthed up ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)). The NAS gives similar advice, suggesting you check over tall brassicas such as Brussels sprouts, kale and sprouting broccoli, as they may need staking ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)).

## Winter digging, beds and keeping off wet soil

The NAS describes winter digging as one of the jobs worth keeping ahead of, and gives a simple test for when it can go ahead: "If the soil is neither frozen nor wet enough to stick to the boots, digging can continue" ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)). If the soil clags on your boots, leave it for another day.

On wet days, plenty of growers lay a plank or board along the edge of a bed to stand on, so that their weight is spread out rather than pressed into one spot. It's an old allotment habit and saves a lot of trampling.

The NAS also says "small, regular bursts of activity at the allotment are much better than prolonged irregular sessions" ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)). Good jobs for those short visits, from the same page:

- turn compost heaps
- move manure into heaps around the plot, ready for digging in
- clean out water barrels
- collect pea-sticks
- when the soil is dry enough, carefully hoe through rows of overwintering veg such as onions, garlic and spring cabbage

([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec))

If you're planting new trees or bushes this month, the RHS says to make sure the ground is not too wet or frozen, and not to plant if it is waterlogged or frozen ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)).

## Fruit: winter pruning apples and pears

The RHS December page says "Now's the time to prune apples, pears, quinces and medlars" ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)), and the NAS also suggests continuing to prune fruit trees, especially apples and pears ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)).

The RHS guide to winter pruning says it should be done while the tree is dormant, after leaf fall and before growth starts again, usually between November and early March ([RHS winter pruning, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/fruit/apples/winter-pruning)). Its main steps are:

1. Start by removing any dead, diseased and damaged shoots and branches.
2. Remove crossing shoots that are rubbing together.
3. Prune out strong shoots growing towards the centre.

The RHS adds that routine winter pruning should remove about 10–20 per cent of the canopy ([RHS winter pruning, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/fruit/apples/winter-pruning)). It's easy to get carried away on a crisp morning with sharp secateurs, so stepping back now and then to look at the whole tree is time well spent.

## Sheds, tools and planning for next year

The RHS suggests cleaning bamboo canes and storing them in the shed or another dry place so they're in good condition for next year. Broken or rotted canes can be shortened for re-use where possible ([RHS, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/grow-your-own/in-month/december)). The NAS says December is a great time to thoroughly clean greenhouses, polytunnels and cold frames ([NAS, checked 19 October 2026](https://thenas.org.uk/monthlyjobsdec)).

Beyond that, December is a good month for jobs most of us put off: wiping down and oiling hand tools, sorting out the seed tin, throwing away labels that have faded beyond reading, and sketching next year's beds on the back of an envelope. ## Small stocking fillers for allotment growers

Small, useful things go down best with plot holders. For a fuller round-up, including bigger presents, see our guide to [Gifts for Allotment Gardeners: Christmas Presents a Plot Holder Will Actually Use](/guides/gifts-for-allotment-gardeners/). Here are some smaller ideas.

**Seedballs.** The Woodland Trust's gift round-up (dated 13 November 2025) lists seedballs packed with native wildflower seeds, from £6 ([Woodland Trust, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2025/11/top-10-green-gifts-for-gardeners/)). They're a nice one for a wildlife corner of the plot.

**Garden twine.** The same post lists twool twine, which it describes as naturally coloured, biodegradable and made from the wool of Dartmoor sheep, at £7.95 ([Woodland Trust, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2025/11/top-10-green-gifts-for-gardeners/)). Nobody on an allotment ever has quite enough twine.

**Hand tools.** The Woodland Trust lists hand tools at £9.99 each, or £7.50 each when buying two or more ([Woodland Trust, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2025/11/top-10-green-gifts-for-gardeners/)).

**Hand cream.** The Woodland Trust also features a beeswax hand cream that it describes as free of palm oil and parabens ([Woodland Trust, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2025/11/top-10-green-gifts-for-gardeners/)).

**Gloves.** A good pair of gloves always gets used. The gardening gloves in the Woodland Trust post are £18, so perhaps one for a bigger stocking ([Woodland Trust, checked 19 October 2026](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2025/11/top-10-green-gifts-for-gardeners/)).

The Woodland Trust prices above are those given in its November 2025 post, so they may have changed since. Check them before you buy.

**Seed packets.** A couple of packets of something they haven't grown before, such as an unusual bean or a different leek, makes a cheerful stocking filler that gets them planning for spring.

**Plant labels.** Labels go missing, snap or fade every season, so a fresh bundle along with a pencil or marker that won't wash off is always handy.
