The village hall is booked, the cow costume is out of the loft and someone is still learning the slosh scene. If you're in this year's show, directing it, running the prompt corner or driving a young chorus member to rehearsals, opening night brings a few questions. Should you buy presents? Who should get a card? And how do you manage it on a big cast without spending a fortune?

## Opening-night cards and gifts: where to start

Our suggestion for an am-dram company is simple: make a card the heart of it, and treat any present as optional. Nobody in a village panto should feel they have to spend money to take part, and a short, personal note about something you enjoyed working on together is a lovely thing to receive.

A few first-night messages survive in theatre archives as individual examples. The University of Sheffield Library holds one 1978 "First night card sent by Keith Dewhurst to the cast of the Cottesloe Theatre production" ([Sheffield archive record](https://archives.shef.ac.uk/repositories/3/archival_objects/21937), checked 18 October 2026). A V&A blog post about the Royal Court Theatre archive shows a "First night telegram to Tony Richardson from Peter Hall and Leslie Caron, 1962" ([V&A blog](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/museum-life/discovering-the-royal-court-theatre-archive), checked 18 October 2026). These are single items in a collection rather than a record of how companies generally do things, but they're a charming glimpse of one card and one telegram sent on a first night.

For a lighter take, the West End Producer's comic column on the Nick Hern Books blog jokes that "There was a time when all that was expected was a card. And in some companies this is still okay." ([Nick Hern Books blog, 2013](https://nickhernbooksblog.com/2013/11/28/west-end-producer-the-secret-to-first-night-presents/), checked 18 October 2026). It's written for laughs, not as guidance, but one line is worth pinning above the dressing-room mirror: "No one likes a card that reads 'It's been great working with you.'"

**Superstitions.** Theatre has plenty of them, and the best known is the "curse" of the Scottish play. The Royal Shakespeare Company explains that "uttering the play's name aloud in a theatre causes bad luck", and gives the remedy: "Exit the theatre, spin around three times, spit, curse and then knock on the theatre door to be allowed back in" ([RSC](https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/about-the-play/the-scottish-play), checked 18 October 2026). So if you're handing out cards in person, mind what you say in the dressing room. For the card itself, "Break a leg!" across the front makes a cheerful send-off.

**Timing.** One easy plan is to get cards into the dressing room before the half on opening night, so people can read them while they do their make-up. If your company has a habit of its own, follow it. Presents, if you're giving any, can wait for the last night or the after-show party, when people have time to open them and a free hand to carry them home.

## Who to include: cast, crew, band, front of house and chaperones

It's easy to think only of the people on stage. Even the comic column insists: "Never make the mistake of only buying for the cast," listing "backstage crew, wardrobe, dressers, stage-door keepers" and adding "Be certain that no one is left out." ([Nick Hern Books blog](https://nickhernbooksblog.com/2013/11/28/west-end-producer-the-secret-to-first-night-presents/), checked 18 October 2026).

A village or society panto has plenty of people working out of sight, and many of them are volunteers. Before you start writing, draw up a list that includes:

- **Cast**, including the chorus and the junior ensemble.
- **Stage management and backstage crew**: the stage manager, ASMs, set builders and the people doing scene changes in the dark.
- **Lighting and sound**, perhaps one or two people tucked away at the back of the hall.
- **The band or accompanist**, and the musical director.
- **Wardrobe, make-up and props**, who may have been busy long before the first stagger-through.
- **Front of house**: the box office, ushers, raffle sellers and whoever runs the interval teas.
- **Chaperones**, if you have children in the cast, who give up their evenings so the show can go on.

On a large company, one card per department, signed by the whole cast, is a neat way to thank everyone without writing a separate message for every single person.

## Small gifts for a whole company on an am-dram budget

The Nick Hern Books column says "You don't have to buy everyone a different present" and "it can be very sweet and thoughtful if you get everyone the same thing", as long as "you must make the cards personal" ([Nick Hern Books blog](https://nickhernbooksblog.com/2013/11/28/west-end-producer-the-secret-to-first-night-presents/), checked 18 October 2026). Joke column or not, the idea suits a big village cast.

**Agree a per-head figure first.** The simplest group buy is a kitty. One person, perhaps a committee member or the stage manager, collects the same small amount from everyone who wants to join in, then buys for the whole company. Agreeing the amount in advance saves awkwardness. As an example of the arithmetic, if 30 people each put in £2, there is £60 to spend. Pick a figure that suits your group, and make it clear that joining in is optional.

**Ideas that work for a whole company:**

- A shared treat for the green room on opening night, such as a big tin of biscuits.
- A group photo from the dress rehearsal, framed for the hall's wall, or printed small so everyone gets a copy.
- A small matching item for everyone, such as a badge, keyring or pencil printed with the show's name and year, to keep in the bottom of a make-up box.
- Stickers, which are cheap, light and easy to put on a script folder or water bottle. The [Proud Back Half of the Cow sticker](https://www.etsy.com/listing/4595205780) from our A2B Designs shop is one for the company joker.

## Gifts for the dame, the villain and the back half of the cow

Some parts in a panto deserve a present of their own.

**The dame.** The V&A describes "the role of the Pantomime Dame that developed during the 19th century", and says "Today's Dames are men in a stunning array of witty outfits, the more outrageous the better" ([V&A blog, Dames on Display!](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/museum-life/dames-on-display), checked 18 October 2026). Something for the wardrobe could go down well: a ridiculous pair of clip-on earrings, an outsized brooch or a gaudy headscarf to work into a costume change. A card written in the voice of their character is a nice touch.

**The villain.** Whoever gets booed every night has earned a laugh in return. A tongue-in-cheek "Most Hissed" certificate, a moustache comb or a suitably sinister black mug for the green room would all make good jokes.

**The pantomime cow.** The V&A's pantomime cow costume is described as "played by two actors, one in the front half of the costume, one in the rear", with eyes and eyelashes "which can be worked by the actor in the 'front end' to show the animal's emotions". The catalogue adds: "It does not require a great knowledge of animal behaviour to play one half of a cow, but it does need good teamwork and the stamina to work in a full animal skin under hot lights" ([V&A collections](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O98607), checked 18 October 2026). The front half gets the eyelashes; the back half gets bent double and none of the glory. A little recognition goes a long way.

## Thank-you gifts for the director and stage manager

The director and the stage manager tend to be first in and last out, so a thank-you from the whole company makes sense. Gather it with the same kind of kitty as above, and hand it over on the last night or at the after-show party, when they can actually stop and enjoy it.

Ideas include:

- A card signed by the whole company, with a line from each person.
- A framed production photo, or a copy of the poster signed by the cast.
- A voucher for a theatre trip or a meal out.
- Something useful for the next show: a good notebook, a smart pen or a small torch for the prompt desk.

## Christmas presents for the am-dram obsessive in your family

Plenty of families have one: the relative who learns lines at the dinner table and vanishes two evenings a week from the autumn onwards. Some ideas for under the tree:

- **Theatre tickets.** A trip to a professional panto, or a play they've always wanted to see.
- **A sturdy script binder and highlighters** for marking up their lines.
- **A good make-up bag or a tidy box** to keep their kit together between shows.
- **Something to wear to rehearsals.** The [Proud Back Half of the Cow sweatshirt](https://www.etsy.com/listing/4595552642) suits anyone who has spent a season in the rear end of a cow.
- **Books about theatre**, whether a history of pantomime, an actor's memoir or a collection of plays.

**About the amateur theatre bodies.** NODA says it "celebrates, connects and supports theatre groups all across the UK" ([NODA](https://www.noda.org.uk/), checked 18 October 2026). It says its story "begins in 1899", it is Registered Charity no. 1171216, and its About page names 11 regions ([NODA About us](https://www.noda.org.uk/about-us), checked 18 October 2026). The Panto Scripts website's footer reads "© 2026 Panto Scripts and NODA Ltd", and the site lets you filter by cast size, including "14 +" ([Panto Scripts](https://www.pantoscripts.org.uk), checked 18 October 2026). NODA's home page invites groups to "Choose from over 160 scripts from the best traditional panto writers" ([NODA](https://www.noda.org.uk/), checked 18 October 2026). Handy if the obsessive in your family has ambitions to direct next year.

The Little Theatre Guild of Great Britain is a charity registered in England (no. 291330), and one of its aims is "to act as the co-ordinating body for the development of independent amateur theatres" ([LTG Aims, Objectives and Rules](https://littletheatreguild.org/about/aims-objectives-and-rules/), checked 18 October 2026). It says it "currently" has 124 member theatres around the UK, loosely organised into three regions (Northern, Central and Southern) and 16 regional hubs ([LTG Our Theatres](https://littletheatreguild.org/our-theatres/), checked 18 October 2026). If your relative's group runs its own theatre, it may be one of them.
